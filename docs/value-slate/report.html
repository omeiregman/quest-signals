Six seasons with Pinnacle

Checks

    The same edge band at every price

    Bets 3–12% above Pinnacle’s fair price, best of all bookmakers, split by odds. Closing-line value stays positive up to odds of about 15 here, but profit gets far noisier above 4 and the published record lost money there, so the strategy stops at 4.

    How the one-winner rule works

    Split the week’s budget C across picks at decimal odds o₁…oₙ, staking each pick in proportion to 1/odds. Every winner then pays the same amount, and a single winner returns the whole budget when

    1/o₁ + 1/o₂ + … + 1/oₙ ≤ 1

    With equal stakes the condition is stricter: every pick needs odds of at least n. Either way, fitting more picks under the rule forces longer odds, so the chance that every pick loses never falls much below one in three.

    The rule changes how you win and lose, not how much you win on average. The average return of a slate is the budget times one plus the average edge. All long-run profit has to come from the edges being real.

    Is there value to find?

    Pinnacle’s prices fade out of the data from late 2025, so for the two newest seasons the referee is Betfair Exchange, which is only reliable in liquid markets. The question is whether flagged “value” bets are real. Here every flagged bet is a flat €1 single (one per match). Profit is noisy; closing-line value settles faster. A real edge shows up as CLV clearly above zero, more than two standard errors.

    Where the longshot trap bites

    The same flagged bets split by odds, four bookmakers, edge of at least 1%, thin exchange markets allowed. The one-winner rule pushes picks toward longer odds, which is where flagged value turns into losses against the closing price.

    How sharp is the reference price?

    A value bet is only as good as the “true” price it is measured against. Median bookmaker margin on match result (1X2) before kickoff, 2025/26. A margin near zero means a tight, trustworthy price.

    Betfair Exchange is near-fair in big leagues but wide and stale in thin ones, so the replay only trusts exchange prices with a margin of 3% or less (except the “Thin markets allowed” strategy). Pinnacle’s figures fade out by February 2026 and look stale, so from 2025/26 on Pinnacle is not the referee; for 2019/20–2024/25, when they came from Pinnacle’s live feed, it is.

    What others have found

    A separate research pass read the published evidence and re-ran the key numbers. Full notes with every source: docs/value-slate/research.md. Each point below says where it comes from.

    1. Value betting against a sharp price has worked, at short odds. Joseph Buchdahl published 20,380 live value bets (2% or more over de-vigged Pinnacle, 2015–2025). Under odds 4 they returned +4.4% (15,385 bets); at odds 4 and above they returned −0.7% against +5.4% expected. Since 2022 the short-odds bets still made +4.2%, while long odds lost 13.1%. Re-computed here from his public archive.
    2. The best-known academic test bet short. Kaunitz, Zhong & Kreiner (2017, arXiv 1710.02824) beat the bookmakers’ average price by a 5-point probability gap. Their bets averaged odds of 2.30. Real-money results were +8.5% over 265 bets, not statistically significant, and bookmakers limited their accounts within months.
    3. Longshots are overpriced, and more so at soft books. Across the same 22 leagues, the lowest-probability bets lose about 17% at closing odds against about 2% for favourites (Hegarty & Whelan, IJF 2025). Our recomputation on 2022–26 closing prices: Bet365 loses 11% at odds 4–6 and 21% at odds 6–10.
    4. Edge size matters at both ends. In Buchdahl’s archive, 2–3% edges returned almost nothing (+0.1%), 3–8% edges did best, and edges over 8% lost (they sit at long odds or on stale prices). Treat anything above about 15% as a data error.
    5. Stake much smaller than feels natural. For a weekly slate the growth-maximising total stake is about 1.25 times the edge: 2.5% of bankroll a week at a 2% edge, under 4% at 3%. Staking twice that wipes out growth, and because the edge is uncertain, half of it (1.5–2% a week, about €15–20 on €1,000) is the safer setting. At a true 2% edge, €100 a week from €1,000 carries about an 18% chance of going broke within three seasons; €50 a week, about 2% (Monte Carlo in the research notes; see Baker & McHale 2013 on shrinking stakes when the edge is uncertain).
    6. Winning accounts get limited. UK Gambling Commission data on 14.9 million accounts: restricted accounts were 1.8 times as likely to be in profit, and most restricted accounts could stake under 10% of the normal maximum (UKGC). No independent evidence was found on how Lithuanian books treat winners, or on their margins.
    7. Judge by closing-line value. Proving a 3% edge from profit alone takes about 9,000–22,000 bets at odds 3–6. Closing-line value tracks long-run profit almost one-for-one and needs a few hundred bets at short odds (Buchdahl).

    What would change the verdict

    1. Lithuanian bookmakers’ own prices. This data only has UK bookmakers, the most efficient soft books in Europe. If 7bet, Betsson LT, Topsport or Optibet are slower or looser, there may be far more value. Testing this needs their historical odds: OddsPapi’s historical endpoint covers 7bet.lt, Betsson and Pinnacle in one call (an ODDSPAPI_API_KEY in the environment).
    2. Prices through the week, not one Friday snapshot. Value usually appears for minutes or hours when a soft bookmaker lags a market move. A single snapshot per match misses most of it; the live poller (two polls a day today) needs to run more often to catch it.
    3. Positive closing-line value over a few hundred real bets. That is the gate before raising stakes. The project’s live poller already records it.
